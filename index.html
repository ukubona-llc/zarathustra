The original SNAP pentad grouped "Basal Ganglia / Thalamic / Tectospinal" as Layer II and "Occipital Cortex / Corticospinal" as Layer V. These are not natural groupings. They conflate where signals go with what executes them, and they scramble the time-scale hierarchy across the five layers. This page is the non-lossy corrected record.

The original mapping — what was wrong

Layer II as stated: "Basal Ganglia / Thalamic / Tectospinal" — conflates a gate (BG), a relay (Thalamus), a spatial processor (Colliculus), and an output wire (Tectospinal tract) into a single bucket without distinguishing their roles or timescales.

Layer V as stated: "Occipital Cortex / Corticospinal" — conflates the terminal sensory witness (V1–V5, visual witnessing) with the voluntary motor output (M1 + Corticospinal). They complete different arcs and run in parallel after L4, not in sequence.

The colliculus was placed in the wrong layer — it is L2 (first processor), not L1. The Spinothalamic tract is L1 (transmitter, not processor).

Layer I · Tensor · Sensation

Spinothalamic tract —
a highway, not a processor

L1 · Tensor
Spinothalamic tract
5–30 ms transit · θᵗ

This is purely a highway, not a processor. Nociception, temperature, crude touch — first-order neurons in the dorsal horn decussate immediately, then ascend contralaterally all the way to the thalamus (VPL nucleus). It carries θᵗ: the raw event stream. No computation happens here. The signal is timestamped and transmitted.

Dorsal horn Spinal cord laminae I–V
First synapse. First-order afferents (Aδ, C fibres) enter here. Cell bodies of second-order neurons. The signal origin — not a processor, a relay node that immediately hands off upward.
Decussation Anterior white commissure
Second-order axons cross the midline within 1–2 spinal segments of entry. Contralateral ascent begins here. This crossing is why right-sided injury produces left-sided pain loss.
VPL nucleus Ventroposterolateral thalamus
Terminal destination of the spinothalamic tract. Third-order neurons depart here for somatosensory cortex (S1). The thalamus at this point is receiving, not gating — gating belongs to L2.
The colliculus does not belong here. That was the original conflation. The superior and inferior colliculi are midbrain processors that receive sensory input and compute spatial maps — they are the first computational layer (L2), not a transmitter. The Spinothalamic tract is a transmitter: it moves signal, it does not process it.

Occipital neuralgia maps here, not to the occipital cortex. The greater, lesser, and third occipital nerves arise from C2/C3 spinal roots and supply sensation to the back of the scalp. They share only a geographic name with the occipital cortex (visual processing). In the SNAP framework, occipital neuralgia is a corrupted event stream at L1 — a persistent high-magnitude error injected into θᵗ. It is θᵗ + ε_nociceptive. The signal is false (no structural damage) but loud, which forces L2 to allocate massive salience budget to it, distorting L₀+Σwᵢ·Lᵢ and hijacking higher layers even though there is nothing to neutralize.

Layer II · Matrix · Attention

SC / IC / Basal Ganglia / Thalamus —
salience, gating, orienting

L2 · Matrix
Superior Colliculus · IC · Basal Ganglia · Thalamus
10–200 ms · L₀+Σwᵢ·Lᵢ

These four structures all participate in the salience and gating layer, but they are doing different things at different timescales. Grouping them as "Basal Ganglia / Thalamic / Tectospinal" obscures this. The Tectospinal tract is not a structure in this layer — it is the output wire from the Superior Colliculus to the spinal cord, and it belongs here only as the motor effector of L2's orienting decision, not as a computational unit.

Superior Colliculus SC · midbrain tectum
Spatial orienting. Builds a 2D priority map of where attention should go. Drives saccades and the tectospinal head-turn reflex. This is where Kubona (orienting) lives — the camera pan. It fires before the cortex knows anything. The SC receives input from the retina, auditory system (via IC), and somatosensory system, and it integrates them into a unified spatial salience map. When a sudden loud noise arrives, it is the SC that asks: "Should we point the cameras there?" and immediately executes the answer via the Tectospinal tract.
Inferior Colliculus IC · midbrain tectum
Auditory integration. Builds the 3D spatial map of sound sources that feeds the SC. The startle pathway runs: cochlea → CN VIII → cochlear nuclei → IC (spatial map) → SC (orienting decision) → Tectospinal tract → cervical cord → SCM / splenius / trapezius. No cortex involved at any step. This is the fastest complete sensorimotor loop in the human nervous system for a complex stimulus — PFC excluded, hippocampus excluded, no NDJSON lookup, pure FastAPI.
Basal Ganglia BG · striatum / GPi / SNr
Action selection via disinhibition. Not a sensor — a gate. The BG's job is to suppress all motor programs except the currently selected one. It operates on a slower loop than the colliculi (tens to hundreds of ms) and requires cortical input to function fully. It belongs in L2 but at the slower end of the L2 timescale. The BG also participates in habit formation — the progressive compression of SlowAPI decisions into FastAPI automaticity. γ|ε_FGT|² → L₀+Σwᵢ·Lᵢ runs through the BG.
Thalamus VPL · MGN · LGN · Reticular nucleus
Relay and filter. Every sensory modality (except olfaction) passes through the thalamus before reaching cortex. VPL for somatosensory, MGN for auditory, LGN for visual. The reticular nucleus gates what gets through — this is L2's attention budget in hardware. The thalamus also participates in arousal and consciousness via the thalamocortical oscillation system. When the thalamus goes offline (deep anaesthesia, certain thalamic strokes), the cortex is effectively disconnected from the world.
The Tectospinal tract is not a computational structure — it is the output wire from SC to the cervical spinal cord, executing the head-turn reflex by firing the contralateral SCM, ipsilateral splenius capitis, semispinalis capitis, and trapezius. The deep suboccipital muscles (obliquus capitis inferior, rectus capitis posterior major) fine-tune the rotation. This is L2's motor arm, not a separate layer.

Layer III · Vector · Intention

Hippocampus —
the biological NDJSON ledger

L3 · Vector
Hippocampus
seconds–years · f(σ²,λ,ε)

Episode formation, spatial mapping, and indexing. The hippocampus does not decide anything — it records and retrieves. It is the biological NDJSON ledger: State_t = Σ Event_i. Place cells encode spatial context. Time cells encode temporal position within an episode. Sequence replay during sleep consolidates the index into long-term cortical memory.

The hippocampus answers one question for L4: "Have I seen this movie before?" It supplies the world model context that PFC needs to compute a counterfactual. Without it, PFC has no history — it is a fresh main.py with no import of prior state.

CA1 / CA3 / DG Hippocampal subfields
CA3 encodes patterns rapidly (one-shot learning). DG (dentate gyrus) performs pattern separation — distinguishing similar events so they are stored as distinct NDJSON lines rather than collapsed into one. CA1 performs pattern completion — retrieving the full episode from a partial cue. Together: write, separate, complete.
Place cells Spatial indexing
Fire when the organism is in a specific location. Provide the spatial coordinate of each episode. In the digital twin: the equivalent of a geographic tag on each NDJSON event — not just what happened, but where.
Time cells Temporal indexing
Fire at specific moments within a timed interval, regardless of spatial position. Provide the temporal coordinate. Together with place cells: every episode has a when and a where. This is the hippocampus's timestamp mechanism — the biological equivalent of the t and iso fields in UkubonaEvent.
Sharp-wave ripples Offline replay
During sleep and rest, the hippocampus replays compressed sequences of the day's events — sometimes in reverse order (reverse replay), sometimes accelerated 10–20×. This is the biological equivalent of replay.py: streaming the ledger from line 0 to rebuild State_t. Consolidates important events into neocortex for long-term storage.
The hippocampus operates on a seconds-to-minutes timescale for encoding, years for retrieval. This is precisely why it cannot intervene in the tectospinal reflex (70–120 ms) and why Enaj's ten-month trajectory was invisible to five physicians who never queried it. The ledger existed in her body's event stream — it was simply never indexed, replayed, or queried. Each visit was a fresh context with no hippocampal import of the prior trajectory.

In the Nietzschean framing: the hippocampus is where blood writes become retrievable. "He that writeth in blood and proverbs doth not want to be read, but learnt by heart." The hippocampus is the organ of learning by heart — not the chart, which can be rewritten, but the episode index, which is append-only at the cellular level once consolidated. The scar remains.

Layer V · Scalar · Action — the deepest clarification

Occipital Cortex + Motor Cortex —
two completions, two arcs, one layer

This is where the original SNAP mapping had the deepest confusion. "Occipital Cortex / Corticospinal" as a single layer implies they are one thing in sequence. They are not. They are two parallel completions of two different arcs — one sensory, one motor — that both happen to terminate after L4 has done its work. They belong in the same layer designation not because they are the same thing, but because they are both outputs of the processed signal.

This is voluntary, cortically-initiated movement — entirely different from the tectospinal reflex (L2). The Corticospinal tract is L5b because it originates from M1, which requires L4 (PFC) to have first composed the voluntary movement. It shares a spinal cord destination with the Tectospinal tract (L2) but differs by an entire cortical loop in latency — ~200–400 ms total for voluntary movement vs ~70–120 ms for the tectospinal reflex.

Timescale: 20–50 ms from M1 firing to muscle contraction; total voluntary reaction time ~150–300 ms including PFC planning.

Why they share a layer designation

The two completions are placed in L5 together not because they are sequential or causally linked, but because they are both the terminal outputs of the full processing chain. After L4 (PFC) has computed its response, two things happen in parallel: the visual system witnesses where the camera landed (Va), and the motor system executes the decided action (Vb). Seeing and acting are both completions. They just complete different loops.

The critical asymmetry: Va (occipital cortex) can operate without Vb (motor cortex) — you can witness without acting. Vb requires L4 to have first planned — you cannot voluntarily act without PFC composition. Va feeds back to L4 (informing the next cycle). Vb is the output of L4's decision. They run in parallel, not in series.

Voluntary movement execution. The two key corrections

1. Colliculus is L2, not L1. The spinothalamic tract is a transmitter (L1). The colliculi are the first processors — they compute spatial maps and generate motor commands. Moving SC/IC from conflation with "BG/Thalamic" into an explicit L2 position with its own computational description is the primary anatomical correction.

2. L5 splits into Va (sensory arc) and Vb (motor arc). These run in parallel after L4, not in sequence. Occipital cortex witnesses. Motor cortex + Corticospinal acts. They share the L5 designation because both are completions — not because they are the same process or causally linked to each other.
